Color semantics / mathematical optical model
Chromatic resonance is derived from the line, not painted onto it
Each line segment takes its base semantic hue from the recipe, then shifts lightness, chroma and hue using the local tangent, discrete curvature, normalized arc position, and primitive phase. The default perceptual encoding is OKLCH (computed and gamut-mapped to sRGB). The selected observer projection is then applied to linear RGB. Thus a change in the geometry changes the color field reproducibly without changing the underlying curve.
u = arc length / total length
κ̂ = clamp(abs(turn angle) / (π · arc step), 0, 1)
h = h₀ + A · [0.60 sin(2πu + φ) + 0.40 sin(2θ − φ)]
L = 0.73 + 0.06 cos(θ) + 0.04κ̂
C = 0.09 + B(0.035 + 0.085κ̂)
OKLCH(L,C,h) → linear RGB → observer projection → sRGB
The expressions describe a deterministic aesthetic mapping, not a physically measured radiometric or neural model. Controls change display appearance only; the base recipe remains unchanged. SVG and PNG exports use the selected projection.

Grammar specification / verifiable and finite
Seven families, five relations, three continuous parameters
The canonical grammar supports one to three curve generators per lexical glyph. Each generator has a family, normalized scale, origin, rotation, and phase. Pairwise relations are computed from geometry, not inferred from the drawing's resemblance to a familiar object.

Encoding convention. Position and scale are relative to a unit glyph canvas (±1); phase and rotation are radians; hue is a normalized 0–360° value. Contours are traced in local parameter t ∈ [0,2π]. An optional pen-phase changes the starting point but not the underlying closed curve; shape rotation changes its orientation. Intensity scales linearly with radius; hue represents an affective temperature band, not a unique word ID. Each catalog entry is a precisely specified design in a starter lexicon, not a proof of universal semantic coverage.


Mathematical and cardinality boundaries. A classical planar cycloid, curtate cycloid, and prolate cycloid do not return to their starting points after a full period: their center advances by 2πa. For closed glyphs, this implementation joins a complete classical cycloidal arch with its reflection across the endpoint chord; the resulting closed cycloidal contour contains two true trochoid arcs and is counted as one cycloidal generator with an explicit closure operation. This is a constructed closure, not a single unmodified classical cycloid. The two requested poetic examples also exceed the three-generator lexical budget. They are therefore marked compositions, not falsely certified as canonical one-to-three primitive glyphs.


Complete expression versus unambiguous decoding. With continuously variable real-valued parameters, infinitely many glyph specifications are possible, so there is ample representational capacity. But injective mappings from all human meanings to glyphs do not follow from curve geometry alone. Universal expression requires a defined ontology, lexicon expansion rules, an interpretation model, and tests for collision, learnability, and disambiguation. This prototype supplies the generator, a compositional grammar, exact sample recipes and renderer; it does not claim to have solved semantic universality.


Morph control. Blending is a visual crossfade between two independently valid glyph executions. Intermediate frames are not asserted to be eighth-family primitives or canonical encoded words. SVG and PNG export the selected source recipe, not the blended preview.
Implementation. SVG paths are generated directly from sampled parametric equations, with no imported fonts or pictorial assets. The construction traces circular and triangular frames alongside each closed trochoidal perimeter; the path is concealed until drawn. Paired example curves are geometric mirrors. Phase drift is applied as a gradual rotation (because for a closed periodic curve, reparameterizing t alone changes only where the pen begins, not the finished silhouette). Exported SVGs contain no text labels.


Trochos — parametric reference implementation. All artwork is generated algorithmically in-browser; no external libraries or assets.


Cultural variations / separate from the language
Shared mathematical forms, distinct cultural readings
Trochos 2.0 uses its own modern visual grammar. Similar circles, intersections and triangles occur in many traditions, but those visual resemblances do not establish shared meaning, a single origin, or a universal sacred language.
Historical contexts and approximate timeline
Classical Greek geometry: circle, triangle and polygon constructions codified in classical mathematical traditions, without a single religious interpretation.
Christian medieval art: the mandorla or almond-shaped aureole appears in religious imagery; meaning depends on the particular image and period.
Islamic artistic traditions, medieval to early modern: geometric star and polygon networks differ across regions, materials, and workshops.
South Asian tantric traditions, medieval to modern: named yantras, including forms of the Śrīcakra, have practice-specific diagrams and meanings; a generic interlocking-triangle pattern is not equivalent.
Contemporary sacred geometry: overlapping-circle motifs are commonly given modern esoteric readings that should not be retroactively claimed as universal ancient doctrine.
Reference: Metropolitan Museum of Art — Geometric Patterns in Islamic Art. Other contexts require source-specific study and historically precise attribution.
